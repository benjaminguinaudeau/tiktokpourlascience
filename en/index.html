Did TikTok fuel the violence of autumn 2026 in France?

In autumn 2026, France saw two social movements: a high-school student movement, with school blockades on 1 October, then a general mobilisation on 17 October, with demonstrations marked by major clashes. The authorities then considered temporarily suspending TikTok. We analysed nearly one million public videos.

The study

Nearly one million videos, from 1 September to 29 October

Several hundred high schools blockaded on 1 and 2 October, between 550,000 and 800,000 demonstrators on 17 October. We studied the videos posted from France about these mobilisations. Their audience follows these two waves.

  • 964,000 videos
  • 16 billion views
  • 4.5 million comments
Read the full note (PDF, 30 pages)

Findings

Three arguments against social media, tested on TikTok's public videos

Three arguments were put forward to justify restricting social media. Here is what we found.

1 Was TikTok used to coordinate the violence?

Calls to mobilise circulated widely. Calls for violence, hardly at all.

Locations mentioned in advance, referrals to private messaging apps, identical messages posted simultaneously: such traces are rare.

Fewer than 0.03% of comments refer to a private messaging group.

2 Did images of violence dominate?

Images of violence account for 14% of views, less than demands or humour.

A video can fall into several categories, so the shares do not sum to 100%.

The visibility of violence is due first and foremost to the media.

“Small creators” have fewer than 50,000 followers; “others” are influencers, anonymous pages and other accounts.

Under videos showing violence, comments condemn these acts far more often than they celebrate them.

Explicit calls for violence make up 0.27% of all comments.

3 Was the violence artificially amplified?

Showing violence is associated with fewer views for small creators, but more for the media.

Content labelled as AI accounts for less than 3% of views. None of our six indicators reveals any large-scale interference operation.

What these results do not say. They cover public videos only. They do not rule out what happens in private exchanges or personalised recommendations. That is why we need your watch history.

Sharing your history

What you share, and how

  1. You request a copy of your data from TikTok.
  2. You upload the ZIP file to the University of Zurich platform. It is read in your browser.
  3. You see exactly what would be transmitted and tick what you agree to donate. Nothing is sent before you agree.

What we receive

  • The list of videos shown to you since 1 January 2026 (link and date).

Optionally, and separately: liked videos, favourite videos, username, answers to a few short questions.

What we do not receive

  • Your searches
  • Your messages and comments
  • Your contacts, date of birth or location
  • The content of your videos

These data never leave your device.

Good to know

  • Open to people aged 18 or over. Participation is voluntary; you can stop at any time.
  • Your data are transmitted and stored encrypted on a University of Zurich server in Switzerland. Raw donations are deleted by 31 December 2027.
